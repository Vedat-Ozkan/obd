# Physics, not prompts, carries the hard ML

Yes, the project can involve genuinely hard applied ML, but almost none of it is fine-tuning, and none of it needs an LLM. With one Equinox EV, the hard problem is that no ground-truth capacity label exists. The work is building an independent capacity measurement from partial Level-2 charges, putting an honest error budget on it, and tracking it with calibrated Bayesian uncertainty. Beside that sit per-cell-group analytics across the 80 series voltages, and a fault detector tested on physically injected faults. Each of these is a real estimation, identification, or anomaly-detection problem, the kind battery-ML job postings describe ("reconcile field data with aging models"). The one-car constraint also forces the evaluation discipline that AI-engineering hiring voices say they value most. Deep fine-tuning is mostly the wrong tool here. Time-series foundation models lose to tuned gradient boosting at small sample counts, and LoRA fine-tuning can make them worse on short series. Published field SOH and fault models use 106 to 515 vehicles. Supervised deep fault detectors reach only about 0.70 AUROC even with 347 cars. The LLM layer is real engineering only as a measured system with a deterministic number check, a judge calibrated against labels, and CI regression. A distilled on-device model is an optional side experiment. The credible showcase is three write-ups, a reproducible pipeline from immutable recordings to metrics, and a small, documented, consented Ultium dataset, which as far as these notes found does not exist publicly.

## Labels, not model capacity, are the binding constraint

Every serious piece of field battery ML runs into the same wall: a car never reports its true capacity. Field studies make their "SOH labels" by **ampere-hour integration over charging segments with OCV–SOC correction**. Single-segment labels are noisy enough that papers take the median over about 30 days or average per about 1,000 km ([Nature Comms / PMC11779878](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11779878/)). The data itself is described as having "random charging and discharging patterns, low fidelity, low sampling frequency, unlabeled data, and outliers" ([eTransportation 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2590116825001018)). The learned SOH models built on such labels use fleets: **106 EVs and 17,729 charging cycles** ([eTransportation 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2590116825000785)), **300 EVs** ([Penn State](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/decoding-battery-aging-in-fast-charging-electric-vehicles-an-adva/)), **464 EVs and more than 1.2 million snippets** ([Tsinghua, Part 2](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2590116824000511)), and at the industry end, Recurrent's **50,000+ vehicles** ([Recurrent](https://www.recurrentauto.com/for-owners)) and Aviloo's FLASH model trained on "tens of thousands" of full drive-down tests ([Aviloo](https://aviloo.com/en-us/aviloo-certified)).

With n=1 there is no held-out vehicle. A "SOH from features" regressor trained on one car would be learning labels that the pipeline itself produced. The signal is also small against the noise. Recurrent reports roughly **1–2 %/yr** range loss ([Recurrent](https://www.recurrentauto.com/research/how-long-do-ev-batteries-last)). Simple arithmetic shows that a 1 %-SOC endpoint error over a 40 % ΔSOC charge already gives about 2.5 % capacity error. So within a portfolio timeframe, the single-session noise floor will likely exceed any real fade. This reframes the project. The hard, showable work is the estimator, its error budget, and its calibrated uncertainty, not a claimed degradation trend. There is one subtle trap. Dividing integrated current by the BMS's own SOC change is largely circular, because the BMS SOC is itself coulomb-counted against the BMS's own capacity belief. That ratio reads out the BMS's capacity number; it does not measure capacity independently. SOC endpoints therefore have to come from relaxed cell-group voltages mapped through an OCV curve, and that curve must be learned from the car's own slow charges and long rests, since no public Ultium OCV curve or cell datasheet was found.

The closest published analogue to this project is one car, not a fleet. Stanford and Volkswagen studied **one EV over one year with 1,655 signals**, extracting health indicators and correlating them with charging habits and seasonal temperature. They showed that lab testing and real use do not line up ([Pozzato, Onori et al., Joule 2023](https://www.cell.com/joule/fulltext/S2542-4351(23)00316-1)), and the data is public ([Mendeley](https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/7vdkzpnjgj/2)). Aitio and Howey inferred health and end of life from field V, I, and T alone, using Gaussian processes ([Joule 2021](https://www.cell.com/joule/fulltext/S2542-4351(21)00532-8)). Those two papers set the template: physics-anchored estimation plus probabilistic modelling on a single system's own data.

## Four hard workstreams one car can honestly support

The notes converge on four pieces of work that are feasible now, each non-trivial, each with an evaluation that does not overclaim. Some numbers below are derived from arithmetic rather than sourced, and they must be checked against recordings: roughly **300 Ah per series group** (88 kWh / 80 groups / ~3.7 V), L2 charging at **about C/11**, and **about 8.7 % SOC per hour**. Resolution comes from the decoded PIDs: current at 0.05 A, cell-group voltage at 0.1 mV, and the energy counter at 0.01 kWh.

**1. An independent partial-charge capacity estimator with an error budget.** The core is Q̂ = ∫I dt / ΔSOC, with SOC endpoints anchored to relaxed voltages. The ΔE-counter/ΔSOC ratio serves as a cross-check, and the BMS readout serves as a comparison, not ground truth. The hard parts are the error budget: current-sensor offset and gain bias, SOC-endpoint error, and timestamp jitter from sequential polling. Session selection matters too, because ΔSOC should be ≥30–50 % to stay under a year of fade. Field literature warns that the voltage window moves between sessions, so "the capacity used in a charging segment might not represent the pack's full usable capacity" ([J. Energy Storage 2023](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2352152X23005078)). The start SOC matters as well: profiles starting at 5–20 % or 50–70 % SOC show lower error than those starting at 30–40 % ([PMC13396333](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13396333/)). Incremental-capacity analysis (dQ/dV, dV/dQ) is a plausible secondary feature, because slow, near-constant-current L2 charging should move cell voltage many 0.1 mV steps per minute. The field literature, however, calls IC peaks fragile under low sampling and fragmented charges ([PMC12056397](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12056397/)). ICA therefore needs resampling onto a uniform voltage or charge grid, Savitzky-Golay or spline smoothing, only the constant-current portion before the taper, and temperature binning. No paper was found that does ICA on Ultium at this resolution, which makes it a real experiment rather than a replication. The honest accuracy metric is **repeatability across sessions under matched conditions**, plus agreement with occasional near-full 10→100 % charges that have long rests at both ends.

**2. Resistance as DCIR at a fixed Δt, not a full equivalent-circuit model.** Online ECM identification (RLS with forgetting, joint or dual EKF) is well established ([Beckers et al., arXiv:2410.03528](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.03528); [Plett 2004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpowsour.2004.02.033)). The papers that separate R0 from RC time constants, though, sample at ≥1 Hz and sometimes as high as 10 kHz ([WEVJ 14(7):168](https://doi.org/10.3390/wevj14070168)). At 0.1–8 s with current and voltage polled as separate, non-simultaneous PIDs, the identifiable quantity is a lumped ΔV/ΔI over 2 s or 10 s, normalized by SOC and temperature. The concrete work has four parts: find clean current steps (charge start and stop, and driving acceleration or regen for better SNR), estimate the I/V timestamp skew by cross-correlation, poll only I and V_pack during resistance-focused logs, and optionally run RLS on a 1-RC ARX form with the known errors-in-variables bias stated. A **hybrid ECM plus a small learned residual** (GRU or MLP on temperature, SOC, and current history) extends this. It is the one place where training a neural model on one car is sound, because the target is voltage, which is dense and self-labeling. It is evaluated by held-out-session voltage RMSE and by how stable fitted R0 stays at matched temperature and SOC. A recursive GP that jointly learns ECM parameter dynamics is the research-grade version ([Aitio et al., arXiv:2304.13666](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.13666)).

**3. Per-cell-group analytics and fault detection, with the other 79 groups as the reference population.** This is the richest signal in the project, and it sidesteps n=1: healthy series cells see the same current, so their voltages cluster, and outliers flag the faulty cell ([Sci. Rep. 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-82960-0)). Field-validated methods include entropy with z-scores on Beijing platform data ([Wang et al., Applied Energy 2017](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0306261916319262)), K-means with Fréchet distance ([ACS Omega 2022](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/acsomega.2c04991)), and correlation of neighbouring voltage differences ([Energy 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360544224023478)). They were validated on data **sampled every 10–30 s** under China's GB/T 32960 ([PMC9648163](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9648163/)), which this project's polling matches or beats. Concretely, the work has four strands:

- **Capacity ranking.** Take the slope of V_i − V_median against charge during constant-current charging.
- **Resistance ranking.** Regress each group's deviation on pack current. The slope estimates extra series resistance, the zero-current intercept captures sensor offset, and intercept drift over time separates a constant sensor offset from a growing drift, following [US 12442868](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/12442868).
- **Self-discharge and early internal-short screening.** Track rest-voltage drift relative to the median, and apply the remaining-charge-capacity method, which turns the gap between adjacent charges into a leakage current ([US 11867765](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11867765)).
- **Robust anomaly statistics.** Use median/MAD z-scores, EWMA, and CUSUM.

Adaptive thresholds of this kind caught faults **9–25 h earlier** than fixed thresholds on real vehicles ([J. Energy Storage 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2352152X24004584)).

Evaluation is where the fault work becomes hard. Faults are injected into real healthy logs using ECM physics:

- **Internal short:** a leakage current V_k/R_sc integrated into group k's SOC, swept across decades of R_sc.
- **Capacity fade:** scale that group's SOC per Ah.
- **Resistance or connection faults:** add ΔR·I_pack (for a connection, split across neighbouring channels).
- **Sensor faults:** an offset or ramp on the reading only.

The detector is then measured on **detection probability versus severity, lead time from onset, and false alarms per vehicle-day on untouched real data**, which is the metric set the literature uses ([Appl. Sci. 16(10):5078](https://doi.org/10.3390/app16105078); [arXiv:2506.13394](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.13394)). OSBAD, an open benchmark of 15 battery anomaly detectors that tunes on proxies because labels are incomplete, gives a reference point ([arXiv:2511.01745](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.01745)).

**4. A Bayesian capacity trajectory with calibrated intervals.** Per-session capacity estimates feed a local-linear-trend Kalman filter or a GP over time and Ah throughput. Each session's measurement variance is propagated from workstream 1's error budget, so the interval reflects physics a reviewer can check. A useful correction to the current BM2 plan follows from this. Conformal prediction is increasingly layered on SOH regressors ([arXiv:2603.24475](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24475); [SSRN 7025872](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=7025872)), but its coverage guarantee assumes exchangeable calibration data. One car's time-ordered, season-drifting sessions violate that assumption. For n=1, the posterior should carry the uncertainty, and conformal should serve only as an empirical check with rolling-origin splits, with coverage reported as failing where temperature shifts. No paper was found applying conformal prediction to a single vehicle's field SOH trajectory. That gap is a small but genuine contribution, if it is presented as a finding rather than a guarantee.

## Where fine-tuning fits, and where it is theatre

"Fine-tuning" tempts a portfolio project, but the evidence cuts against it here. A July 2026 break-even study of Chronos-Bolt, Moirai, and Lag-Llama found that **LoRA fine-tuning "can actively degrade performance on short series."** It recommends zero-shot below about 700 training samples ([Tan Jerome & Simon, arXiv 2607.04919](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.04919)). One car yields perhaps tens of capacity estimates per year. An August 2026 review of large models for battery prognostics found that "controlled same-data-budget comparisons against well-tuned specialists remain absent" and that field deployment "remains largely unexplored" ([arXiv 2608.26111](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.26111)). The battery "foundation models" (PBT, pretrained on 977 cells, and Battery-Timer, on 220,153 cycles) target lab cycle-life prediction, not field logs ([arXiv 2512.16334](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.16334); [arXiv 2505.08151](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.08151)). Even PBT's authors say transfer gives only incremental gains unless the pretrained model already encodes relevant aging knowledge. Microsoft's BatteryML benchmarks find linear models on handcrafted features competitive with deep models, and deep models varying widely across seeds ([arXiv 2310.14714](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.14714)).

Training still has a real role, just not deep fine-tuning. It appears in three forms. First, **lab-to-field transfer as a Bayesian prior.** Fit a capacity-versus-throughput prior on public lab cells (via BatteryML), then update it with the Equinox's own estimates, and judge it by whether the field estimates fall inside its intervals. Second, the **ECM-plus-residual network** from workstream 2. Third, a **same-data-budget benchmark** that puts IBM's TTM, which is about 1M parameters, fine-tunable on CPU, and accepts exogenous channels ([HF card](https://huggingface.co/ibm-granite/granite-timeseries-ttm-r2)), against ridge and LightGBM. The expected result is an honest negative: "foundation-model fine-tuning did not beat LightGBM at N samples; here is the break-even curve." That is more credible in an interview than a headline win. Lab-to-field evaluation with several vehicles becomes possible on EVBattery (464 EVs, CC BY-NC-ND 4.0, [arXiv:2201.12358](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.12358)). Those results must stay in a separate table from the Equinox's own.

The LLM side has one legitimate training project: **BM7 distillation**. A 1–3B open model is fine-tuned with QLoRA on reviewed hosted summaries of structured metrics, with grammar-constrained decoding so it can only reference provided fields. It is scored on the existing deterministic number check against the template and the hosted model. The expected honest outcome is that the template wins on faithfulness and the small model wins on fluency and offline use. The value is privacy and offline operation, not accuracy. On-device runtimes (llama.cpp, LiteRT-LM, ExecuTorch) handle models of this size in 2026 ([arXiv 2605.08195](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.08195v1)). Before generating any training data, check the hosted provider's terms on training with its outputs. Compute is not a blocker for any of this. Everything above except distillation runs on CPU. ROCm 7.2.1 under WSL2 supports only the RX 7900 XT/XTX/GRE and 9070 class, not the RX 6600 ([AMD matrix](https://rocm.docs.amd.com/projects/radeon-ryzen/en/latest/docs/compatibility/compatibilityrad/wsl/wsl_compatibility.html)), and a rented RTX 4090 costs about **$0.34–0.69/h** ([RunPod](https://www.runpod.io/gpu-models/rtx-4090)). A $50 cap covers the whole distillation series. For deployment, trees, ridge, GPs with a fixed kernel, and ECMs export to JSON and evaluate in pure TypeScript inside `obd-battery`. A Python-versus-TypeScript parity test at 1e-6 is a cheap, strong verification item. ONNX Runtime in Expo has known config-plugin friction ([issue #548](https://github.com/microsoft/onnxruntime-inference-examples/issues/548)) and is worth it only for a neural model.

## What is out of reach, and what a few beta cars unlock

| Direction | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| EIS / high-frequency impedance | Not feasible | Needs kHz sampling; OBD polling gives 0.1–8 s |
| 2-RC ECM identification | Not feasible (1-RC only on best-rate driving pulses) | RC separation needs samples within the first second of a step |
| DFN/SPMe PINNs, PyBOP electrode-parameter fitting | Not feasible as a deployed model | Published mostly on simulation ([arXiv 2606.28220](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.28220v1)); electrode parameters are unidentifiable from pack-level, quantized, low-rate data. PyBOP ([arXiv:2412.15859](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.15859)) is useful only to show identifiability limits on a 1-RC fit |
| Cross-vehicle SOH regressor | Not with n=1 | No held-out vehicle; labels are self-derived |
| Early-life lifetime prediction à la Severson | Not applicable | Lab fast-cycling protocol, not field data ([Nature Energy 2019](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41560-019-0356-8)) |
| Supervised deep fault detection (DyAD, MCNN) | Not trainable here; usable as an external benchmark | Built on 347 and 515 vehicles; DyAD reportedly reaches about 0.70 AUROC ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37741826/); [MCNN](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-56832-8)). DyAD data has no stated license |
| Self-supervised pretraining on charging snippets | Data-gated | Needs a large unlabeled multi-vehicle set |
| Recurrent/Aviloo-style "compare to fleet of same model" | Not with n=1 | Needs a fleet |

A small fleet of 3–10 Ultium cars changes the evaluation more than the models. It enables **leave-one-vehicle-out evaluation**, a **hierarchical Bayesian capacity model with vehicle-level random effects**, and **split conformal calibrated by vehicle**, where exchangeability becomes defensible. Results should still be reported per vehicle rather than as a pooled RMSE. Beta data also brings obligations. PIPEDA applies to "commercial activity" judged by the nature of the activity, not profit ([OPC](https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/privacy-topics/privacy-laws-in-canada/the-personal-information-protection-and-electronic-documents-act-pipeda/pipeda_brief/)), and a paid inspection service is clearly inside it. The OPC's meaningful-consent guidance calls for granular, dynamic, express consent when data creates residual risk ([OPC](https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/privacy-topics/collecting-personal-information/consent/gl_omc_201805/)). In practice that means:

- a hashed VIN, with the raw VIN never uploaded
- no GPS, and coarsened timestamps in any release
- separate opt-ins for the personal report, research use, and public release
- a dataset license chosen before collection and named in the consent form

Several unknowns remain that no amount of modelling resolves. Nothing public was found on Ultium's balancing strategy, its OCV curve, its module sense-wire topology, or what GM's own BMS capacity and SOH PIDs mean. Temperature PIDs are still unverified on this car. Whether ICA peaks survive this car's polling is an empirical question. Several cited papers were read only at abstract level because full texts were paywalled.

## Showcase it as field-data ML with rigorous evals

The hiring evidence points one way. Hamel Husain argues the bulk of AI work is "setting up experiments to test how well the AI generalizes to unseen data… and designing good metrics" ([hamel.dev](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/revenge/)). Eugene Yan's rubric prizes data literacy, eval understanding, and comfort with uncertainty ([eugeneyan.com](https://eugeneyan.com/writing/how-to-interview/)). Rivian's Senior Battery ML Engineer posting asks candidates to "reconcile field data with aging models" and predict pack lifetime from large datasets, and it does not mention LLMs ([ClimateTechList mirror](https://www.climatetechlist.com/job/rivian-sr-battery-machine-learning-engineer-SPxq2r9QqLUB2T)). A web developer will not out-compete electrochemists on P2D or COMSOL. The realistic lane is **a field-data ML engineer who respects physics**: ECM and PyBaMM as baselines and synthetic generators, with their limits stated.

Ranked by expected signal, the pieces are:

1. **Capacity estimation with an error budget and calibrated, backtested intervals** (BM2 plus the Bayesian trajectory).
2. **LLM eval infrastructure** (BM5). This turns the LLM feature from a wrapper into a measured system: faithfulness scoring, a judge calibrated against human labels with a confusion matrix, prompt-injection cases, and CI replay regression.
3. **Anomaly detection with physics-based fault injection** (BM3). It is only credible with severity curves and a real-data false-alarm rate. Without those it reads as a toy.
4. **On-device parity and drift monitoring** (BM4), strongest if it catches real seasonal temperature drift.
5. **Distillation** (BM7), which is the most common and least differentiated piece.

The MCP relay and the agentic build process are good AI-engineering signal. Frame them as infrastructure that makes the data trustworthy (read-only allowlist, immutable recordings, provenance), not as the ML headline.

Where to show it follows from what makes EVBattery notable: **real field data, a clear task definition, and a clear license** ([arXiv:2201.12358](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.12358)). The bundle is:

- **The BM6 write-ups.** Each covers problem, data, splits, baseline, metrics, coverage, failure cases, and what was NOT RUN. The #1 own-car n=1 case study is explicitly modelled on the Stanford/VW single-vehicle paper.
- **A model card per model and a datasheet for the dataset** ([Mitchell et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.03993); [Gebru et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09010)).
- **One reproducible command** from `fixtures/recordings/` to the reported metrics, using a uv-locked `tools/ml/` workspace, a content-hash or DVC manifest, and a seeded eval that CI reruns on a fixture subset and fails on regression.
- **Real and synthetic results in separate tables.**
- **A short demo video** of the phone reading the car.
- **Once consent allows, a Hugging Face dataset card** for a de-identified Ultium charge-session dataset. None was found publicly, which makes it the single most distinctive artifact the project can produce.

## Conclusion

The worry that this collapses into "fetch data, call an LLM twice" misreads where the difficulty lives. The LLM is the easy layer. The hard part is a measurement problem: making a trustworthy number out of noisy, unsynchronized, low-rate BMS telemetry with no ground truth. That kind of work is squarely what battery-ML teams hire for. The project's most distinctive ML is estimation, system identification, robust statistics, and Bayesian uncertainty, with a thin, checked LLM layer on top. Its most credible "training" results may well be negatives, reported with break-even curves.

Two implications follow. First, BM2 should lead with a physics-propagated Bayesian interval and demote conformal prediction to a rolling-origin coverage check, since exchangeability fails for one car across seasons. Second, the project's strongest long-term asset is not a model but the consented, documented Ultium session dataset and its reproducible pipeline. Models trained on it will be replaced. A clean real-world dataset for a pack architecture with no public data will not.
